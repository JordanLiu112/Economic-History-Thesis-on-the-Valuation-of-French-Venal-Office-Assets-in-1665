For my economic history thesis at the University of Oxford, I conducted original research on the valuation of French venal offices and applied economic principles and asset pricing theories. I created large datasets, charts, and models that approximated the return on investment for different kinds of offices, contributing to existing historiography with new insights backed by hard, verifiable data. I had very amiable discussions on my thesis with my supervisor, Professor David Parrott, a specialist in early modern French history. He helped guide my research, and after reading my final draft, he was very impressed and commented that he was even able to learn new things.

Abstract: The Grand Siècle witnessed a remarkable expansion of the venal office system, in which the French crown conferred upon individuals the right to sell, exchange, and transmit their offices to heirs. Although historians have thoroughly studied this topic, current scholarship lacks large-scale quantitative analysis and the use of modern financial models. This thesis aims to examine the determinants of office prices and assess to what extent venal offices behaved like modern financial assets with economically rational prices. The area of study is France during the reign of Louis XIV, and the main primary source is Colbert’s systematic survey of office prices 
in 1665. This thesis also makes use of the findings of a recent quantitative study by Bonhoure, Musy, and Tallec (2024) and analyses the well-established scholarship on venal offices by Guy Rowlands, William Doyle, Mark Potter, Roland Mousnier, Christophe Blanquie, Daniel Dessert, among others; this is essential to understanding the characteristics of venal offices and constructing economic models for office prices. Furthermore, this thesis offers wider implications for the use of financial instruments by modern states, assuming that venal offices were sophisticated enough to resemble modern financial assets.
